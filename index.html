Reading Further

Advanced Strategies for Engaging with Texts

Reading a text is one thing; making full use of its features is quite another.

The practices outlined on this site apply across a variety of media formats, from news stories and social media posts to academic articles and scholarly books. In a world free of gatekeepers and beset by generative text, these strategies serve as additional layers of defense against flawed and faulty information.

West Essex High School Library | Mr. Thompson

Part I
Faulty Information: A Dichotomous Key

What common hazards imperil our information ecosystem? How can we recognize them?

Faulty information comes in two primary forms.

There’s misinformation, which is false information shared inadvertently or accidentally. It may be incomplete, inaccurate, or misleading; it can also be completely wrong. Though it can cause damage, misinformation isn’t deliberately malicious. Indeed, people often spread it without realizing what they’re doing.

Then there’s disinformation, which is false information created and spread with the explicit intention of causing harm. Its consequences are typically economic, social, and/or political, as disinformation aspires to manipulate opinions, drain pocketbooks, incite conflict, and redirect human attention.

In a nutshell, misinformation results from human error while disinformation stems from deliberate deceit. Unfortunately, misinformation and disinformation mix together in our digital media environment, where honest mistakes and malevolent actors blend in an informational slurry.

Let’s consider a few examples to better understand the distinction.

Example I | Breaking News

Exhibit A | Wire | Breaking! | Slug: shooting-devel

Imagine a breaking news story about a mass shooting. Consider potential information sources: statements or emergency announcements from the institution (school, mall, office, etc.) where the shooting is occurring, bulletins from local emergency services (police, EMTs, radio transponders, etc.), social media posts from people on the ground, and, eventually, stories composed by news media and journalists (professional and amateur). Nobody has a perfect view or complete understanding of what’s happening; a coherent narrative takes time to emerge.

A well-meaning television news broadcaster may share information from the police department live on-air. If an aspect of that information is flawed—say, a detail about the shooter’s present location—that’s misinformation, an inaccurate but well-intended attempt to inform the public about what’s going on.

If, to the contrary, a social media account shares a gory AI-generated photo and claims it was taken live on the scene, that’s disinformation: a malicious lie meant to provoke an emotional response and mislead the public.

Example II | Academic Publishing

Exhibit B | Journal | Peer Reviewed | Status: under review

Next, imagine a paper published in a prestigious scientific journal. In theory, it’s been through round after round of ethical and peer review and represents the authors’ best understanding of their subject. Perhaps it’s a study of a new treatment for a rare disease.

Say, for the sake of argument, that a critical mistake in the authors’ arithmetic calls their findings into question. If nobody catches it, that’s misinformation: slipping up regarding a fact. This is unsettling, but it does happen.

Likewise, if a popular website writes an article pumping up the paper, mistake unbeknown, that’s misinformation, too: spreading a flawed study while ignorant of said flaw.

Ideally, somebody will catch the error and inform the authors/journal. They can either issue a correction (assuming the study’s findings hold up after the proper value is calculated) or a retraction (if the study’s findings no longer seem so sure).

If, on the other hand, the authors or publisher received this feedback from a cautious reader and failed to act on it, it becomes disinformation: deliberately spreading a lie for career advancement, professional prestige, or to protect one’s reputation.

(What if the authors still hold a genuine belief in the efficacy of their findings? Misinformation? Disinformation? Something else entirely? Honestly, it’s hard to say. These are the perils of the scientific method and any attempt at a dichotomy.)

Taking things one step further, if a social media user is unaware of a retraction and shares the paper, that’s just misinformation: a case of somebody not knowing any better.

Does the distinction matter? Absolutely! To make a tortured analogy, misinformation is an “Almost there!” message sent from traffic; disinformation is an “Almost there!” message sent from bed. It’s all about intention: the former means well whereas the latter is an outright lie.

Part II
Reading Within the Text

How do you establish your bearings within a text?

Stated vs. Implied Thesis

Station 2 | Within the Text Itself

Academic writing and certain media formats (books, essays, etc.) often contain an explicit thesis that identifies the main idea or primary argument behind a piece of writing. Careful readers know there’s often more to the story, but the author clearly intends that specific hypothesis to serve as the backbone of their work. We call this a “stated thesis.”

Likewise, with a little effort from the reader, media that doesn’t contain a blinking, blaring, obvious thesis is still driven by a central idea. Instead, readers must make an inference based on the text itself. Get in the habit of identifying this “implied thesis” to better engage with any given source.

How do you find an implied thesis? Look for repeated words, terms, phrases, or, more abstractly, ideas. If something’s coming up over and over, it’s likely part of a central argument. Keep an eye peeled for similar examples and details as well as patterns in evidence. Mind authorial tone and mood, as the way a person reacts to their subject can help you determine their perspective.

Exhibit C| Short-Form Video

Imagine a short-form video in which a person recounts three experiences: a negative customer service experience with a beaver, a frightening traffic incident with a nutria, and a telemarketing scam run by a muskrat. You might infer an implied thesis that has something to do with prejudice toward semiaquatic rodents.

Exhibit D| Swift, 1729| Two Readings

Implied theses are also a favorite tool in satirical writing. In Jonathan Swift’s A Modest Proposal (opens in a new tab), the author suggests that poor Irish people can partially alleviate their plight by selling their children (as food) to the wealthy. The text itself is deadpan and driven by that explicit thesis, even containing recipes and arithmetic to support its argument. However, its absurd premise and preposterous examples hint toward an implied thesis that’s deeply critical of regressive taxation, mercantilism, and an Irish upper class complicit in British colonial rule. An implied thesis gives sustained irony its bite.

Part III
Reading Around the Text

How do you establish your bearings beyond a text?

Lateral Reading

Station 3 | Hit Pause; Seek Additional Sources

Lateral reading is a practice of carefully reviewing a source, then looking beyond it to evaluate its claims.

First, skim the source. Think about authorial purpose, the plausibility of claims, the quality of evidence cited, and your own emotional response. If you have doubts about its credibility, don’t spend too much time on the source itself.

Instead, step away and seek other coverage of the topic. Type the author’s name or the name of the source itself into a search engine and see what comes up (adding a second search term like “background,” “credibility,” “funding,” or “legitimate” if necessary).

(Nota Bene: I can’t stop you from using large language models—what society as a whole identifies as AI—when you evaluate a source, but I can encourage you to actually follow through by clicking on the links the generated text cites. Please note that you’re adding an additional item to your To-Do list!)

Review your results carefully! Don’t settle for an LLM overview or click the first link. Scroll and consider the quality of the new sources you’re considering. Ask questions about the original source author’s background and motivation. Are they an authority on the subject? If not, what qualifies them to speak on it?

Finally, if the author or source seem bona fide, seek other coverage of the topic. Compare perspectives in your results and look for points of agreement.

If you find yourself unsure about the quality of a source, do a bit of lateral reading and make a judgement.

Instrument | Station 3
Establish Your Bearings!

One source cannot fix a position. Record what you find from three, then determine whether they seem to agree on the same basic points. If your sources disagree, this is as much a success as a complete affirmation. You’re doing the work!

Checkpoint A | Who Published This?

Can you identify the publisher, the site owner, or the organization behind it? Be sure to record where you found this info!

Checkpoint B | Who Really Funds This?

Follow the money; try to establish ownership. Who pays for this? What else do they pay for?

Checkpoint C | Who Talks About This?

Find additional coverage of the same subject. Does it agree? Does that information stem from an independent source?

Bibliographic Mining

Station 4 | Take It Back Now, Y’all

Bibliographic mining is a simple, oft-neglected strategy that makes use of the otherwise asinine practice of citing one’s sources.

Review the bibliography, works cited page, or references at the end of a text. If one seems useful (or suspicious!), try to locate it yourself. (If you’re having a hard time, this is when your librarian can provide vital assistance.)

This is how researchers build out source webs; it’s also, in theory, how the disingenuous are exposed.

Instrument | Station 4

List what your source cites, then try to actually find the cited material! How many of them can you actually locate? What’s the true functional value of this bibliography?

Corrections and Retractions

Station 5 | Take Two: Corrections and Retractions

This skill specifically applies to academic journals and news articles.

Sometimes, writers make mistakes. Sometimes, writers lie. In either case, if a publisher values their readers, they’re likely to issue a correction or retraction.

In news media, corrections may be added in the form of an explicit correction section, as an update to an article or, alas, silently without direct acknowledgment. If a news publisher issues a correction, it usually appears at the top or bottom of an article.

Full retractions, in which a publisher realizes a story is critically flawed and acts to remove it, are somewhat rare in news media, but, when they do happen, the original article tends to disappear. It may or may not be replaced by an acknowledgement.

In academia, journals have different policies for corrections, but they generally fall into three categories. A “correction” addresses errors that do not overturn the findings. An “erratum” fixes a mistake the journal itself made (e.g. a typo or printing problem that alters a table). A “corrigendum” amends an author’s mistakes (a miscalculation or failure to disclose important information).

A full retraction occurs if an article’s results are deemed unreliable or if misconduct comes to light. Publishers usually issue a separate retraction notice and flag the original paper. Academic retractions tend to occur without much fanfare and move rather slowly.

Part IV
Passing Judgement

Where do your sources align? Where do they disagree?

Fact-Checking

Station 6 | The Plot

Fact-checking, that much-contested term, is the process of verifying claims, quotes, and evidence by locating it in its original context and/or comparing it to other information, including the general scholarly consensus, about a subject.

It may alarm you to hear that some news organizations and book publishers (especially large nonfiction houses) do not employ staff factcheckers, instead relying directly on journalists and authors to vet their own material. Proper fact-checking is sometimes challenging, always time-consuming, and often maddening, but it provides a foundation upon which all human knowledge is built.

Fact-checking is not “doing your own research”; rather, it’s relying on constructed knowledge, ideally from experts and professionals, to determine whether a claim is very likely, merely plausible, or rather farfetched.

When dealing with news media, sites like Snopes (opens in a new tab), FactCheck.org (opens in a new tab), and PolitiFact (opens in a new tab) can provide helpful information. For more academic matters, lateral reading and bibliographic mining are more likely to pay dividends.

Three Cs for Evaluating Sources

Use this framework to quickly evaluate a potential source. Let the questions below guide you, but don’t answer all of them!

Creator

Who created this source? Is an author or authorial group identified? What are the creator’s academic and/or professional credentials? Are they a recognized expert in their field? Who or what makes them an authority? Is the creator affiliated with institutions or organizations? What other connections are revealed by a cursory search?

Content

Does the content provide ample attributions and citations? Are its sources easy to locate and verify? How does this information square with your prior knowledge about and other sources on the subject? Is it reasonable? How is the material written and organized? Whom is it addressing? What questions does it strive to answer? What positions does it take? When was the content last updated? When was it first created?

Context

Where, why, how, and by whom was this content disseminated? Who funded its creation? Where and how did you find it What other materials exist alongside it? Can you locate reviews of the content or its creator? Why does it exist? Whose agenda(s) does it serve?
